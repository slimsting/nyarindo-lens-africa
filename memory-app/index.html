A SMALL PRACTICE OF ATTENTION

Meet this moment
where it is.

Two forms of journaling live here, with different purposes. One clears the lens without keeping a record. The other builds a record you can return to.

TWO JOURNALING PRACTICES · ONE DISTINCTION

Clear the lens first. Then return to the pattern.

They can support one another, but they are not the same practice and should not be merged.

FIRST TYPE

Journaling Without Witness

Private, unstructured writing that is never reread. Write whatever is actually there without organizing it, concluding for it, or looking for insight.

  • Use it to clear the fog of performance.
  • Do not show the page to anyone.
  • Close without reading it back today.
  • This is not a record for comparison or analysis.

Source: (book 1, pages 12–13)

SECOND TYPE

The Returning Journal

Structured writing that is kept, revisited, and accumulated over time. Each entry asks the user to separate the bodily signal, their word-based signal report, and the interpretation or story attached afterward.

  • Use timestamps and the same structure across entries.
  • Write without forcing a conclusion.
  • Return later to notice patterns across the accumulated text.
  • The aim is seeing, not fixing or maintaining a streak.

READ · NOTICE · RETURN

Stories from the spiral.

These six lived vignettes are from Teaching the Wave Mind, Book Six, “Lived Vignettes.” (book 6, pages 10–11) ↗ They are stories to sit with, not labels to place on yourself or anyone else. Open one if it helps; you can also begin without a story.

The student who stopped asking questions

A child loves science. They ask endless questions. Why is the sky blue? How do birds know where to fly? What is electricity? The teacher says: We do not have time for that. Stay on topic. The child stops asking. The questions do not go away. They go inside. The child learns: curiosity is dangerous. My expansion is not welcome. The pulse contracts and never releases. The child becomes a black hole for their own curiosity.

Another teacher hears the same child. They say: That is a great question. Write it down. We cannot answer it today, but we will come back to it. The child feels seen. The question is held. The child learns: curiosity is welcome. I can wait. Contraction can be temporary. The question returns weeks later. The child answers it themselves.

The artist who stopped creating

A young painter shows their work to a parent. The parent says: That is nice, but what will you do for a living? The painter stops showing. The paintings become secret, then rare, then absent. The painter says: I am not creative. The pulse contracted and never released. The painter became a black hole for their own art.

Another parent sees the same paintings. They say: This is beautiful. Keep going. We will figure out the money later. The painter feels held. The contraction of practicality does not crush the expansion of creation. The painter continues.

The partner who stopped speaking

In a relationship, one person says: When you raise your voice, I feel small. The other says: You are too sensitive. The first person stops speaking their needs. They become quiet, agreeable, hollow. The relationship looks peaceful. It is a black hole. Signals go in. Nothing returns.

Another partner hears the same words. They pause. They say: I did not know. Thank you for telling me. I will try differently. The first person feels heard. The contraction was received, not ignored. Repair is possible. The relationship is not a black hole.

The employee who stopped innovating

A worker has an idea. They share it in a meeting. The manager says: That is not how we do things here. The worker stops sharing. They do their job, nothing more. Their creativity goes into silent resentment. The pulse contracts permanently. The worker becomes a black hole for their own innovation.

Another manager hears the same idea. They say: Tell me more. The worker explains. The manager says: We cannot implement it now because of X, but let us keep it on the list. The worker feels seen. The expansion was not rejected. It was held. The contraction was not needed.

The believer who stopped questioning

A person grows up in a religious community. They have doubts. They ask a leader: What if the story is not literally true? The leader says: Doubt is sin. Do not ask that again. The person stops asking. The doubts become shame. The person performs belief. Inside, they are hollow. They become a black hole for their own questioning.

Another community hears the same question. The leader says: Many people have asked that. Let us sit with it. The person feels safe enough to doubt out loud. They deepen into their faith, not despite the doubt, but through it. The contraction of doubt was held, not punished.

The wave mind in the linear classroom

A child thinks in spirals. They make connections across subjects. In math class, they raise their hand and say: This reminds me of the way leaves grow on a branch. The teacher says: That is off topic. Focus on the problem. The child learns: my connections are wrong. My expansion is not welcome. They stop raising their hand. The wave is still there, beneath the surface, but it cannot reach the air. The child becomes a black hole for their own pattern recognition.

Another teacher hears the same child. They say: Tell me more. How are leaves like math? The child explains. The teacher listens. Then the teacher says: That is called pattern recognition. It is a gift. Now let us see if the pattern holds across these three examples. The child feels seen. The wave is not punished. It is invited. The child's expansion is held by the container.

00

BEFORE THE NEXT STORY

Begin with how the last story felt.

Come back to your own felt experience first. After you carried the story, did you feel relief, tension, heaviness, lightness, confusion, calm, nothing in particular, or something else?

Then add anything that happened afterward. Your feeling and the event are both observations; neither proves that the story caused the other.

01

THE POINT OF ATTENTION

Where will attention land?

Which feeling label seems closest?

These are interpretations offered as starting points, not measurements of the signal. Choose none if none fit.

02

THE RAW REPORT

Raw feeling first. Story second.

First, the body. Notice what is present before the mind explains it: pressure, heat, tingling, heaviness, movement, stillness, sound, or nothing you can name yet.

Then, your description. The body does not send a sentence. You are translating what you notice into human words. Describe the feeling itself before deciding what it means.

03

THE STORY ARRIVES

Catch the meaning your mind attached.

A story is the interpretation added after the sensation. It may say what the feeling means, what it predicts, who caused it, or what it makes you want to do.

Write the first story as it arrived. You are observing the interpretation, not swearing that it is true.

04

THE PAUSE

Let the first story wait.

You do not have to choose yet. The first story may be accurate, partly accurate, or only a familiar route. Let the signal remain unnamed for a breath.

05

A STORY WORTH CARRYING

Choose one account you can test.

06

BACK INTO THE WORLD

Make the next turn small enough to take.

Leave the entry here. Continue the practice across ordinary days. The next turn will begin by asking how this story felt afterward—not by asking you to prove it true.
